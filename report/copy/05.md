### shadow-h2
Do transients avoid Earth’s shadow?

### shadow-intro
An object in orbit is visible only via the sunlight it reflects. When orbiting objects pass into Earth’s shadow, they should therefore go dark.

VASCO considered objects in geostationary orbit (36,000 kilometres up), where a single orbit takes one day and a satellite stays above a point on the equator. At this height, Earth’s shadow covers a disc of sky {n:c.shadow_diam} degrees across, centred on the point opposite the Sun. If some transients are glints from objects in orbit, the part of a plate inside the shadow should show fewer transients than the same area of sky elsewhere. VASCO reported such a shortfall in their own catalogue.

In their test, VASCO placed the shadow as seen from the centre of the Earth. The telescope, however, sat on the Earth’s surface, 6,400 kilometres from its centre; from this point of view, an object 36,000 kilometres up appears in a different direction. On plates Earth’s shadow reaches, the shadow falls {n:sh.geo.shift_lo} to {n:sh.geo.shift_hi} degrees from where the Earth-centred frame puts it. In our analysis, we therefore place the shadow as seen from Palomar.

Seen from Palomar, the shadow also moves during an exposure: as the Earth turns, the observatory travels more than 1,000 kilometres in 50 minutes, and the shadow’s edge slides {n:sh.geo.sweep_lo} to {n:sh.geo.sweep_hi} degrees across the plate. A glint lasts a fraction of a second, occurring at an unknown moment during the exposure. Therefore, we count each transient according to the share of the exposure that its plate location spent in shadow.

The shadow’s edge is also not sharp: between {n:sh.geo.umbra} and {n:sh.geo.penumbra} degrees from its centre, the Earth obscures only part of the Sun, and a glint there would be dimmer. Following the shadow through each exposure requires accurate plate times. Since our calibration to asteroids fixes the median plate’s time to within {n:times.precision} minutes, the shadow is placed to better than a tenth of a degree.

Flaws in the photographic emulsion, dust on the glass, and marks from copying have no connection with where Earth’s shadow fell. If significantly fewer transients fall inside the shadow than the differences between plates can explain, this could indicate objects in orbit. With the few plates that Earth’s shadow reaches, such a shortfall must be judged against how far the count can vary by chance.

### repeat-title
Repeating VASCO’s measurement

### repeat-text
VASCO’s shadow test (Villarroel et al. 2025) counts the transients that fall inside Earth’s shadow, and compares the count with uniform control positions placed on the same plates. We first repeat the test as VASCO made it: the plates of their listed observing nights, their time for each plate, the shadow seen from the centre of the Earth with a sharp edge at a single instant, and each transient counted once.

With the public catalogue and control positions, {n:rep.vasco_obs} of the {n:rep.vasco_n} transients fall in shadow, against {n:rep.vasco_exp} expected from the controls, a ratio of {n:rep.vasco_r}. VASCO’s binomial model puts this shortfall at {n:rep.vasco_z} standard deviations. Their paper reports 349 transients in shadow, 180 control positions per plate, and 7.6 standard deviations; the public files hold 100 control positions per plate and give a larger count in shadow, and we have not found the cause of the difference.

We then change one step at a time ({fig:fig.vasco_repeat}). Seen from Palomar rather than from the centre of the Earth, the ratio becomes {n:rep.palomar_r}. With every plate of the catalogue assigned its corrected date and time, it becomes {n:rep.times_r}. With the shadow moving across each plate during the exposure, its edge following the share of the Sun’s disc that the Earth hides, and each transient counted by its share of the exposure in shadow, it becomes {n:rep.moving_r}. Counting each transient by its rating, as VASCO’s 2026 analyses do, gives {n:rep.rated_r}, which the binomial model puts at {n:rep.rated_z} standard deviations.

The binomial model treats every transient as independent. As shown above, the number of transients on a plate depends on the plate itself, so a count drawn from the few plates the shadow reaches varies far more than the model allows. Shuffling the real plate times among the plates, which moves the shadows onto other plates, shows how far the count varies by chance. Shuffled times give a ratio at least as low as {n:rep.rated_r} about {n:rep.shuffle_p}. When each transient counts fully, rather than by its rating, shuffled times also give a ratio at least as low as {n:rep.moving_r} about {n:rep.shuffle_p_raw}.

The pooled count also depends on which plates the shadow happened to fall on, and whether those plates are rich or poor in transients. In the rest of this section, we therefore compare shadow with sunlight on the same plates.

### shadow3-title
Searching for a deficit in Earth’s shadow

### shadow-model-text
We treat the transients on a plate as two populations. The first is a background of artefacts, such as flaws in the emulsion, dust on the glass, or marks from copying, which Earth’s shadow cannot affect. If some transients are glints from objects in orbit, those glints would be spread evenly over the plate, since where they fall has no connection with the plate itself. Such transients would vanish wherever their location fell in Earth’s shadow.

The test therefore counts the transients that fall in shadow on each plate, and compares the count with the number expected if Earth’s shadow made no difference. We express the result as a ratio: the transients observed in shadow, divided by the number expected.

With no shadow effect, the ratio should be 1. If some transients are glints that vanish in the shadow, the ratio falls below 1, but by less than the glints’ share of the total transients. This is because the expected count is derived from the number of transients recorded on the plate, and that number already lacks the glints that vanished in shadow ({fig:fig.glint_model}).

### model-caption
(a) {n:model.art} artefacts, which are denser towards the edges as observed on real plates ({fig:fig.vasco_edge_map}). (b) {n:model.glints} glints spread evenly, making up {n:model.share} of all transients. (c) In Earth’s shadow there is no sunlight to reflect, so the {n:model.lost} glints in that area never appear (hollow circles). (d) The transients recorded in shadow, divided by the number expected there with no effect: {n:model.oe0} with artefacts alone, {n:model.oe} with the glints.

### shadow-ratio-text
Transients falling in the region where the shadow passed during the exposure count towards both shadow and sunlight. For example, if a transient is located somewhere that spent one third of the exposure in shadow, that transient counts one third towards shadow and two thirds towards sunlight.

Even with no shadow effect, the shadowed part of a plate need not hold a share of its transients equal to its share of the area, because transients grow denser towards the edges of most plates. This rise is part of the background, whether it comes from the plates themselves or from how the catalogue was built; glints, which have no knowledge of where plate edges fall, cannot produce it. An expectation based on area alone could thus find an excess wherever the shadow covered an edge, and a shortfall wherever it covered the middle.

We therefore take the expected share from the plates that Earth’s shadow never reaches. Averaged together, these plates provide a map of where on a plate transients tend to lie (see {fig:fig.vasco_edge_map}). For each plate the shadow did reach, we lay its shadow on this map: the share of the map’s transients that falls under the shadow is the share of the plate’s transients expected in shadow. Plates with no parts falling in sunlight throughout the exposure, or with less than 1% of their area in shadow, offer no useful comparison and are left out.

We then separately sum the observed and expected counts over the plates, and take their ratio. Each plate’s expected count comes from its own number of transients, so a plate that is rich or poor in transients overall cannot by itself create a shortfall. Plates with more transients in their shadowed area carry more weight, and plates with little area in shadow count for little.

This comparison is a standard statistical method, known as a score test (Berman 1986, Applied Statistics). {fig:fig.shadow_forest} shows the ratio measured for each plate, and for all plates together.

### shadow4-title
Finding the true uncertainty

### shadow-null-text
Across the {n:sh.plates} plates, {n:sh.obs} transients fall in shadow, against {n:sh.exp} expected, a ratio of {n:sh.oe}. Counting statistics, which allow for each transient’s rating and each plate’s total but treat every transient as independent, would put this shortfall at {n:sh.z} standard deviations. However, transients are observed to clump within plates, so the count in any one part of a plate varies far more than counting allows.

To measure the true uncertainty, we moved each real shadow onto other plates that Earth’s shadow never reached, which were chosen to hold a similar number of transients at a similar declination, since transients spread differently over plates at low declination. These {n:sh.replicas} replicas keep the specific shadow geometries, but apply them to transients with real clumps and edge effects on plates where Earth’s shadow never fell, so they can contain no shadow effect.

Repeating our measurement on these replicas gives a median ratio of {n:sh.null_median}, with the middle 68% of results falling between {n:sh.null_q16} and {n:sh.null_q84}. Shortfalls at least as large as the one observed in the real data are encountered by chance around {n:sh.p_t}.

As a check, shuffling the real plate times among the plates, which moves the shadows elsewhere, gives a shortfall this large about {n:sh.perm_p_t}. Such shuffles introduce shadow geometries that do not match the real measurement, so we judge the measurement against the replicas above instead.

To set a limit, we added simulated glints to the replicas, as in {fig:fig.glint_model}: spread evenly over each plate, and absent wherever their location was in shadow. We repeated this for glints making up a range of shares of the transients. When glints make up {n:sh.limit95} of the transients, only 5% of replicas give a ratio of {n:sh.oe} or higher, so larger shares are ruled out at 95% confidence. A ratio of {n:sh.oe} is thus consistent with no glints, but also with glints making up as large a share of the transients as shown in {fig:fig.glint_model}.

Each plate’s time is known only to within the range that its own asteroids, or the clock offset measured for its year, allow. Drawing every plate’s time many times from that range moves the ratio by {n:sh.sys}; the limit set above accounts for this systematic uncertainty.

### shadow5-title
Checking the result

### shadow-checks-text
The verdict holds under every choice we tested, although the observed shortfall rests largely on one plate:

- Without {n:sh.loo.top}, which holds the most transients in shadow and so carries the most weight, the ratio becomes {n:sh.loo.top_oe}; leaving out any other plate gives {n:sh.loo.oe_min} to {n:sh.loo.oe_max}.
- With a flat map, or one built from plates below a declination of 25 degrees, replicas match the shortfall {n:sh.maps_p_t}.
- Choosing replica plates by their number of transients alone, or by the number of transients the pointing model expects ({fig:fig.vasco_pointing}), moves the 95% limit to {n:sh.limit_count} or {n:sh.limit_pointing}.
- Counting every transient fully, rather than by its machine-learning rating, gives {n:sh.raw.oe}, matched by chance about {n:sh.raw.p_t}.
- Retraining VASCO-ML with VASCO’s own code, in {n:ml.runs} runs over {n:ml.sets} settings, gives ratios ranging from {n:ml.sh_min} to {n:ml.sh_max}.
- Fitting each plate’s density of transients, rather than counting them, gives a density in shadow {n:sh.ratio_fit} times that in sunlight, matched by replicas about {n:sh.fit_p_t}. A second fit, which measures the share of transients that are glints, gives {n:sh.share_fit}. The first fit detects added glints about as often as the count, and the second less often.

### pool-title
Pooling the plates

### est-text
As Dr Beatriz Villarroel (VASCO lead) suggested, we also measured the shortfall by pooling the plates, including those wholly or barely in shadow. The pooled count in shadow is {n:est.e2_r} times the count that the pointing model expects there, and {n:est.e1_r} times the count expected from VASCO’s uniform control positions (the last step in {fig:fig.vasco_repeat}).

A pooled count compares the transients in shadow on a few plates with the transients expected from all plates, so its value with no shadow effect depends on how many transients the shadowed plates happen to hold.

To measure this dependence, we repeated the pooled counts on the replicas, which contain no shadow effect. Against VASCO’s control positions, the replicas’ median ratio is {n:est.e1_null_n} when the replica plates are chosen to hold a similar number of transients, and {n:est.e1_null_mu} when they are chosen to have a similar count expected by the pointing model. How often the replicas give a ratio as low as the measured one therefore depends on this choice: from {n:est.e1_p_lo} to {n:est.e1_p_hi} against the control positions, and from {n:est.e2_p_lo} to {n:est.e2_p_hi} against the pointing model.

Most of the pooled shortfall comes from two plates, {n:est.low_plates}. The pointing model expects these plates to be rich in transients, yet they hold only {n:est.low_share} of the number it expects, in sunlight as well as in shadow. Since their shadowed areas are large, they carry {n:est.low_exp_share} of the count the pointing model expects in shadow. Without them, the ratio against the pointing model rises to {n:est.e2_without}, and the ratio against the control positions to {n:est.e1_without}.

A shortage of transients across the whole of a plate cannot have been caused by Earth’s shadow. Choosing replica plates by their number of transients uses each plate’s observed total, which a genuine shadow effect would lower. However, a shadow effect could lower a plate’s total only by the transients lost in shadow, far less than the shortage on these two plates.

Our count compares the shadowed part of each plate with that plate’s own total, so a plate that holds few transients overall does not lower it; without either of these two plates, it stays between {n:sh.loo.oe_min} and {n:sh.loo.oe_max}, as the checks above show. With no effect, its median on the replicas stays between {n:sh.null_med_lo} and {n:sh.null_med_hi}, however their plates are chosen.

Dr Villarroel’s recommendations for replications (VASCO project site, July 2026) ask for normalisation by total counts, and for controls that do not use the observed distribution of transients. On the first, we report the counts normalised by total counts above, but take the count within each plate as our measurement, because the pooled values with no effect depend on which plates are compared. On the second, our map comes only from plates that Earth’s shadow never reaches, so it cannot hold a shadow signal; uniform positions in place of the map, one of the checks above, give replicas that match the shortfall {n:sh.flat.p_t}.

### power-title
Sensitivity and a combined fit

### est-power-text
Dr Villarroel also noted that a comparison within plates sets aside each plate’s total, including that of {n:est.whole_plate}, the plate wholly in shadow, and so may detect a genuine shortfall less often than a pooled count. To test this, we added artificial glints making up {n:est.power_g0} of the transients, and counted how often each measurement found a shortfall beyond what 95% of replicas without glints give (a higher share means a more sensitive measurement):

- Our count: {n:est.z_power} of replicas;
- The pooled count against VASCO’s control positions: {n:est.e1_power};
- The pooled count against the pointing model: {n:est.e2_power}.

The pooled counts indeed detect the added glints more often. However, a measurement counts as detecting glints only when it passes a threshold set by the replicas without glints. For the pooled counts, this threshold shifts depending on how the replica plates are chosen, as shown above; by contrast, the threshold for our count does not. With so few plates, every measurement misses glints making up {n:est.power_g0} of the transients more often than it finds them.

Our count cannot use {n:est.whole_plate}, the plate wholly in shadow, because no part of it stays in sunlight for comparison. A pooled count can use its total instead. However, {n:est.whole_plate} holds {n:est.whole_ratio} times the number of transients the pointing model expects, so its total shows no shortfall either.

Dr Villarroel also suggested combining the comparison within plates with each plate’s total, judged against the pointing model, so that every plate the shadow reaches contributes. This combined fit gives a density in shadow {n:est.h_r} times that in sunlight; replicas give a value this low about {n:est.h_p_lo} to {n:est.h_p_hi}. This method detects added glints in {n:est.h_power} of replicas, against {n:est.z_power} for our count. The plate totals thus add little information, because a plate’s total typically differs from the pointing model’s expectation by about {n:est.disp}.

### shadow-verdict
Across the {n:sh.plates} plates with areas both in shadow and in sunlight, {n:sh.obs} transients fall in shadow against {n:sh.exp} expected, a ratio of {n:sh.oe}. Replicas of the measurement on other plates give a shortfall at least this large about {n:sh.p_t}, so no statistically significant shortfall is found in Earth’s shadow. The shortfall comes largely from one plate, {n:sh.loo.top}; without it, the ratio is {n:sh.loo.top_oe}.

The pooled counts give lower ratios, {n:est.e2_r} against the pointing model and {n:est.e1_r} against VASCO’s control positions. However, their values under no effect depend on which plates are compared, and most of their shortfall comes from two plates that hold few transients in sunlight as well as in shadow (an effect Earth’s shadow cannot cause). Without these two plates, the pooled ratios are {n:est.e2_without} and {n:est.e1_without}.

Glints making up more than {n:sh.limit95} of the transients are ruled out at 95% confidence. With only {n:sh.plates} plates, smaller shares cannot be ruled out.
