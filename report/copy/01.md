### hero-title
Examining the evidence for glints from orbit in 1950s sky photographs

### hero-lede
Between {n:data.year_first} and {n:data.year_last}, Palomar Observatory in California photographed the sky from the north celestial pole to about 30 degrees south of the celestial equator. Each field was recorded on a glass photographic plate. The plates show faint points of light that have no counterpart in modern sky catalogues. The VASCO project, led by Dr Beatriz Villarroel, has catalogued more than 100,000 of these transients.

Villarroel’s team has proposed that some transients may be produced by sunlight glinting off flat, reflective objects in orbit around Earth in the pre-satellite era. In papers published in 2025 and 2026, the team reported three statistical tests in support of this theory: fewer transients appear inside Earth’s shadow; more transients appear around the dates of nuclear weapons tests; some transients appear along straight lines.

We are grateful to the team for publishing its catalogue and code. Building the catalogue took years of careful work, and its release encourages this kind of re-examination.

Each of the three statistical tests relies on a comparison of transient counts: between locations on the sky, between nights, or against the number of alignments expected by chance. Due to selection requirements applied when creating a catalogue, differences in counts across time and location can arise regardless of any genuine glint signals.

We repeat the three tests on VASCO’s published catalogue, with every plate’s date checked and corrected where required, and times checked against the trails of asteroids. The efficiency of the catalogue’s selection has not been published, so it is not known how often a genuine glint would have been kept at each location on a plate, or on each plate. Each test therefore compares the catalogue with itself: in the shadow test, the shadowed and sunlit parts of the same plate; in the nuclear test, each plate against the count expected from where it pointed on the sky; in the alignment test, the real catalogue against random catalogues built from its own transients. Every result is judged against the variation that the real data show.
