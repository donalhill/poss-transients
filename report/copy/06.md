### align-h2
Do transients fall in straight lines more often than chance allows?

### align-intro
A flat object tumbling in orbit would flash each time it caught the Sun, leaving a line of points during a long plate exposure. We search for such alignments among the {n:al.transients} transients of VASCO’s catalogue that fall within the central 6 x 6 degree regions of each plate.

Given a large sample of unevenly spread points, many will line up by chance. Three questions naturally arise:

- Can the search find alignments in every direction?
- Does the catalogue contain more alignments than expected by random chance?
- How many genuine glint tracks does a fit to the catalogue find?

### align1-title
Searching for alignments in all directions

### align-dir-text
A satellite at geostationary height stays nearly fixed above the observatory while the telescope follows the stars, so on the plate it moves close to east–west. Any line of glints it leaves would therefore lie within a few degrees of east–west; a search for glints must find lines in that direction as readily as in any other.

The same alignment task often arises in particle physics, where the paths of charged particles must be reconstructed from hits they leave in a detector. The simplest tracking method fits a straight line through a set of points, resulting in a track candidate. The quality of each candidate can be assessed by checking how far the points lie from the line of best fit. Such a method works equally well in all directions, including east–west when applied to this setting.

We tested the track search by adding {n:al.dir_tracks} artificial glint tracks to the catalogue at each of {n:al.dir_angles} fixed angles, ranging from east–west to north–south. At every angle ({fig:fig.align_direction}), the search recovers between {n:al.dir_min} and {n:al.dir_max} of them as high-quality track candidates (a track score of 1 in 1,000 or better, as defined below).

### aligndir-caption
The search finds a similar share of artificial glint tracks in every direction ({n:al.dir_min} to {n:al.dir_max}), with no drop in the east–west direction where the glints of a geostationary object would lie.

### align2-title
Fitting every track candidate

### align-fit-text
We build track candidates from pairs of transients up to {n:c.al_lmax} arcminutes apart. Each pair marks the two ends of a track, and the transients lying closest to the line between them are added, giving three to six transients in total. We then fit a straight line through each candidate, and measure how closely its transients follow it.

Two sources closer than about 4 arcseconds (the width of a star’s image on the scans) blur into one, and two sources are separated reliably only beyond about twice that distance. We therefore leave out candidates with two transients closer together than {n:c.al_minsep} arcseconds.

The position of each transient on a scan is uncertain by around an arcsecond. As such, even points from a genuine straight track can stray from a true straight line. Each track fit is scored with a χ² per degree of freedom (χ²/ndof), which compares the points’ actual distances from the line with a one-arcsecond uncertainty. A genuine track gives a value near 1 or below. Points scattered loosely about the line give a much larger value. From χ²/ndof we take the χ² probability, the share of cases in which measurement errors alone would give a fit at least this poor. Only candidates with a χ² probability of {n:c.al_chi2p} or more pass this straightness test and go on to be scored.

A good fit alone does not make a track stand out: in crowded parts of a plate, pure chance can cause transients to align frequently. We therefore assign each track candidate a track score, which measures how easily chance alone could produce it. The score uses four numbers, as {fig:fig.align_method} shows: the candidate’s number of transients (<em>k</em>), its length (<em>L</em>), its width (<em>w</em>), and the number of transients in a box at least {n:c.al_side} arcminutes across around it (<em>N</em>). The lower the track score, the less often chance alone produces such a track. The idea and the formula match those VASCO used to judge their own candidates (Edmunds and George 1985).

Transients often come in close pairs: VASCO’s catalogue holds {n:al.pairs20} times as many pairs of transients within 20 arcseconds of each other as the same plates would hold with their transients placed at random. Two transients this close fix a direction only loosely, so a third lying roughly in line with them makes a short track that looks stronger than it is. Lezama and colleagues (2015) developed a method for finding alignments among clustered points, and we follow it. We count a track’s transients in boxes {n:c.al_box} arcseconds long along the track, about the distance over which close pairs are unusually common, so that transients closer together than this count only once.

Candidates built from a set of six transients also yield candidates composed of any combination of three, four, or five of that set. Where track candidates share a transient, we keep only the best-scoring candidate, such that each transient counts once.

### align3-title
Measuring how often tracks arise by chance

### align-null-text
Any catalogue of transients will hold tracks by chance, so finding track candidates proves nothing by itself. The proper test is whether the real data holds more strong track candidates than chance alone can produce.

This test requires a reliable background-only model: random catalogues that match the real data as closely as possible, except that they contain no genuine tracks. Since transients cluster on plates, and their surface density varies, a uniform random distribution cannot serve as this model.

We therefore created {n:al.copies} random catalogues based on the real data itself. Each random catalogue keeps all of the plates from the real data, including the total number of transients on each and their tight groups of transients, while breaking up almost all alignments. Every lone transient is moved at random by up to {n:al.shift_arcsec} arcseconds; this is enough to break genuine lines, but small enough that each plate keeps its distribution of transients on larger scales.

Transients within {n:al.link_arcsec} arcseconds of one another, either directly or through a chain of neighbours, form a group. Each group is moved as a single piece, turned at random and shifted by up to {n:al.shift_arcsec} arcseconds, and no transient is moved to within {n:al.link_arcsec} arcseconds of another group. Because a group keeps its shape when it moves, a line formed within a group would also appear in every random catalogue, and the comparison could not tell whether it arose by chance. We therefore leave out any track holding three or more transients from one group, both in the real data and random catalogues.

To measure chance correctly, the random catalogues must satisfy two conditions:

- <strong>They must break up real tracks.</strong> To test this, we added {n:al.absorb_tracks} artificial tracks to the real catalogue, one at a time, and moved their transients in the same way the random catalogues move them. Of the candidates these tracks produce with a track score of 1 in 10,000 or better, only {n:al.absorb4} in 100 survive the moving, and so would also appear in the random catalogues; at 1 in 1,000 or better, {n:al.absorb3} in 100 survive. Any genuine excess of glint tracks would therefore still show at {n:al.keep4} of its true size ({n:al.keep3} at 1 in 1,000). The fit we perform below allows for this absorption effect, track by track.
- <strong>They must not understate chance.</strong> If the random catalogues held fewer chance tracks than the real one, chance alone could look like a signal. We check this on the candidates that only just fail the straightness test, with a χ² probability between 1 in 1,000 and 1 in 100. A genuine glint’s track would pass that test, so these candidates are chance alignments, built from the same pairs, groups, and density of transients as the rest. With a track score of 1 in 1,000 or better, the real data holds {n:al.sb_real3} such candidate, against {n:al.sb_copies3} on average in the random catalogues. At 1 in 100 or better, the real data holds {n:al.sb_real2}, against {n:al.sb_copies2} in the random catalogues. Within these small counts, the random catalogues show no sign of holding fewer chance tracks than the real data. Comparing the real data against the random catalogues could thus hide part of a real excess of tracks, but it cannot create a false signal.

Close pairs of transients require one further decision. A close pair could be background, such as a speck of dust that the detection software splits into two, in which case the random catalogues should move the pair as one piece. It could also be signal, such as two glints from one object, in which case the random catalogues should move the two separately.

The two cases cannot be differentiated in the real data, so the analysis should depend on it as little as possible. Counting transients within {n:c.al_box} arcseconds of each other along a track only once, as described above, reduces the dependence without removing it. With this counting rule, random catalogues that move close transients on their own hold {n:al.close_split3} track candidates at 1 in 1,000 or better on average, and {n:al.close_kept3} when close pairs move as one piece. The random catalogues we use move close pairs as one piece, and so hold the fewer chance tracks of the two. Against them, an excess in the real data would stand out more, not less.

The real and random catalogues pass through the same analysis code, so track candidates are found in the same way in both.

### align4-title
Searching for an excess of tracks

### align-text
With the background-only model in place, we compare the track candidates in the real data that pass the straightness test with those in the random catalogues. Genuine glints would appear as candidates scoring better than chance allows, appearing as an excess beyond what the random catalogues predict. We fit the real track score distributions for candidates of 3, 4, 5, and 6 transients ({fig:fig.alignments}), first with the background-only model, then with glint tracks added. A track of six transients also contains tracks of three, four, and five, so the four distributions are fitted together.

To model a genuine glint track, we added {n:al.lib_tracks} artificial glint tracks of each number of flashes to the catalogue, one at a time, each with its flashes spread over 2 to 10 arcminutes, and scored each plate with and without its track. From these, we measure how a glint track would change the four track score distributions.

### align-caption
Comparing the real track score distributions in data (red) with the average of the random catalogues (grey). Across the full range of track scores (lower is better), and for each type of track from 3- to 6-transient, the real data holds about as many track candidates as random chance suggests.

### align-verdict
The fit to VASCO’s catalogue finds no statistically significant excess of glint tracks. For tracks of 3, 5, and 6 transients, the fitted number of glint tracks is zero. For tracks of 4 transients, the fitted number is slightly above zero, but random catalogues give a number at least as large about {n:al.p_4_t}, so it is consistent with chance.

Converting this result into a limit on the number of glint tracks would require the efficiency of the catalogue’s selection: how often a real flash enters the catalogue. VASCO’s catalogue does not record this, so we set no limit on the number of glint tracks.
