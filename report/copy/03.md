### survey-h2
The Palomar Sky Survey

### survey-intro
Images were taken on plates using Palomar’s 48-inch Schmidt telescope, which was built to photograph wide areas of sky. Each plate is a sheet of glass 14 inches square, and covers a patch of sky 6.5 degrees across (about thirteen full Moons). Every patch was photographed through both a blue and a red filter, with red exposures lasting 40 to 60 minutes. Copies of the plates were scanned in the 1990s, and astronomers now work from those scans. One of the red plates is shown in {fig:img:plate_xe344.png}.

### plate-image-caption
Plate XE344 is one of the survey’s red plates. It was photographed on 9 August 1954, and is centred on right ascension 22h 39m and declination +30.8 degrees (the sky’s equivalents of longitude and latitude). Its scan is 14,000 pixels across, each covering 1.7 arcseconds of sky, and is shown here at a twentieth of that resolution.

The brightest star, left of centre, has a faint ring on the opposite side of the centre. The ring is a ghost image of the star, created by light reflecting inside the telescope.

### dates-h2
When was each plate taken?

### dates-text
The date of each plate is usually taken from the digitised archive at the Space Telescope Science Institute. This archive stores dates in short blocks of text called headers, which are attached to each scan.

We have found that around {n:dates.early_pct}% of plates have archive dates which are one day early. Since various statistical tests in the following work rely on knowing when each plate was taken, we have checked the date of every plate and made corrections where required.

### dates1-title
Plate labels

### dates-plate-text
The observer wrote each plate’s number and date by hand, and the writing was photographed onto the edge of the glass. Since observing nights spanned two calendar days, observers wrote both dates; for example, “Nov 29/30 1951”.

Astronomers keep time using Universal Time (the clock at Greenwich), which is seven or eight hours ahead of California depending on daylight savings.

Exposures began after dark in California, when it was already past midnight in Greenwich, so the second date shown on the plates is the correct one. For the two example plates in {fig:group:label-caption-1}, we find that the archive dates incorrectly use the first rather than the second written date.

### label-caption-2

### dates2-title
Using asteroid trails to check dates

### dates-ast-text
Asteroids move relative to stars, typically by around half an arcsecond per minute. During long exposures, asteroids thus appear as short trails, while stars appear fixed as the telescope tracks them. Using NASA’s JPL Horizons service, we can determine the position of every known asteroid on any night in the 1950s with high precision.

For two example plates in {fig:group:asteroid-caption-1}, we show the trail a known asteroid would follow on the archive’s date (red dashed line) and where it would be a day later (red solid line). Over the course of a day, an asteroid moves around a quarter of a degree, so the two positions are well separated on the plate.

Using the archive’s date, we find no trail where the asteroid is predicted to be. One day later, however, we find trails left by asteroids at their expected positions. On the plates we checked, {n:dates.ast_late_trails} asteroid trails across {n:dates.ast_late_plates} plates appear one day after the archive date. On another {n:dates.ast_ok_plates} plates, {n:dates.ast_ok_trails} trails confirm that the archive date is correct, so the method is able to distinguish between cases.

### asteroid-caption-1
Plate XE356, showing asteroid (210) Isabella. The archive date for this plate is 29 November 1951, but the asteroid trail actually appears on 30 November.

### asteroid-caption-2
Plate XE617, showing asteroid (163) Erigone. The archive date for this plate is 8 April 1956, but the asteroid trail appears on 9 April.

### dates3-title
Correcting the dates

### dates-fix-text
Palomar numbered the red plates in the order they were taken. The one-day error affects long, unbroken runs of plate numbers, listed in {fig:fig.dates_table}. Palomar’s own log of the survey also survives. It records each night by its evening date, so the correct date in Universal Time is the day after the log’s date. For some plates, the log’s plate number differs by one from the number on the label, so matching by number can miss a plate or take another plate’s entry. We therefore match each plate to the log based on where the telescope pointed.

For the {n:dates.log_checked} plates also dated by an asteroid trail or a handwritten label, the log agrees on {n:dates.log_agree}. The two exceptions are typing errors in the log, where we take the date on the label.

Where a plate has more than one source for its date, we take the most direct source available, in this order:

- Its handwritten label: {n:dates.by_label} plates;
- An asteroid trail: {n:dates.by_asteroids} plates;
- Palomar’s log, matched to the plate by where it pointed: {n:dates.by_log} plates.

Of all the plates, {n:dates.exceptions} do not follow the pattern of their run, and are listed in {fig:fig.dates_table}. Of these, {n:dates.gross} are wrong by months or years, and {n:dates.one_day} by a single day. Each is corrected from its label, and all of the date corrections can be found in our code repository.

### dates-table-caption
Most archive dates are one day early, spanning long runs of plate numbers. Only {n:dates.exceptions} plates break the pattern of their run, {n:dates.gross} of them by months or years.

### dates4-title
Checking the time of each exposure

### dates-time-text
The Earth shadow test requires the time of each exposure as well as its date. As seen from Palomar, Earth’s shadow at the height of a geostationary orbit (36,000 kilometres up, where an orbit takes one day) moves against the stars by up to about 2 degrees an hour as the observatory turns with the Earth. As such, an error of half an hour in a plate’s time would misplace the shadow by up to a degree.

Each header gives the start of the exposure in hours and hundredths of an hour, rather than in hours and minutes (for example, 06:82 means 06:49). Where the header and the log give different times of day, as is the case for {n:times.apart} plates, we take the time that the plate’s asteroids support. Where the asteroids do not determine the time, we assign the time that is consistent with the rest of the night: the exposure must lie wholly between the end of evening twilight and the start of morning twilight, must not overlap another plate’s exposure, and must preserve the order of the night’s plate numbers.

We then checked every plate’s time against the sky. A known asteroid moves along a predicted track during the exposure, so if a plate’s time is wrong, the asteroid is found displaced along its track by its speed multiplied by the error. The asteroids show that the written times are later than the true start of each exposure by {n:times.late_lo} to {n:times.late_hi} minutes, depending on the year, and by the same amount at every hour of the night ({fig:fig.clock}). The asteroids also reveal {n:times.errors_n} plates whose written time, in the header and the log alike, was wrong by {n:times.errors_min} to {n:times.errors_max} minutes.

We therefore calibrated every plate’s time to the sky. {n:times.own_asteroids} plates contain enough asteroids to take their times from those asteroids, and the remaining {n:times.survey} take the median offset found for their year. After this calibration, the median plate’s time is known to within {n:times.precision} minutes (68% interval).

### clock-caption
Asteroids put the start of each exposure at around {n:times.late} minutes before their written times, varying from {n:times.late_lo} to {n:times.late_hi} minutes between years but not with hour of the night. Single asteroid trails scatter by about {n:times.trail_sd} minutes about their plate’s offset.
