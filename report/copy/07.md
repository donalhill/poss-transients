### nuclear-h2
Do transients cluster around nuclear tests?

### nuclear-text
During the Palomar sky survey, the United States tested nuclear weapons in Nevada and the Pacific. In two papers (Scientific Reports, 2025 and 2026), VASCO report that observing nights within a day of a nuclear test held more transients than other nights. The 2026 paper, which uses US tests, finds that chance alone would give an excess this large in about 1 in 40 cases (p = 0.024). VASCO kept the window of one day either side from the 2025 paper, as a hypothesis set before the analysis. The authors take the excess being confined to such a narrow time window as a sign that it is genuine.

We test the same time window with VASCO’s catalogue of transients, each counted by its rating as in their 2026 paper, and using the full list of US tests from the Department of Energy (DOE/NV-209), which VASCO’s 2026 paper cites. The list in their code leaves out all the tests of Operation Upshot–Knothole, in spring 1953.

VASCO date each night by the local date on which it began. Since that evening is already the next day in Universal Time, their window of one day either side runs in Universal Time from the day of a test to two days after it. We test this window as VASCO applied it, and also a window of one day either side of each test in Universal Time.

### nuc1-title
An excess near tests, and where it comes from

### nuc-why-text
Counted per night, as VASCO count them, the {n:nuc.ctx.nights_near} observing nights near nuclear tests hold {n:nuc.ctx.per_night} times as many transients as other nights. Of the {n:nuc.plates} plates in the catalogue, {n:nuc.plates_near} were taken near tests, and counted plate by plate they hold {n:nuc.per_plate_raw} times as many transients as the other plates. Two features of the survey contribute to this excess.

Firstly, a night’s transients come from its photographs. Nights near tests averaged {n:nuc.ctx.ppn_near} plates, against {n:nuc.ctx.ppn_other} on other nights, and only {n:nuc.ctx.empty_near} of them have no plate in the catalogue, against {n:nuc.ctx.empty_other} of other nights. A night with no plates contributes no transients, even though nothing was observed there. We therefore count transients per plate, not per night.

Secondly, the sky above Palomar changes through the year as Earth orbits the Sun. On spring nights, the telescope pointed away from the Milky Way. There, fewer stars crowd the field, so fewer detections are rejected for lying near a known star, and more survive as transients. Of the plates near nuclear tests, {n:nuc.ctx.spring_near} were taken between February and June, against {n:nuc.ctx.spring_other} of other plates.

We allow for both factors by comparing each plate’s transient count with its expected count, as described above: the fit across all plates of distance from the Milky Way, dust across the plate, distance from the ecliptic, the share of faint Gaia stars the plate records, and whether the plate lies in the equatorial row.

The expected counts are around {n:nuc.ctx.sky_pct} higher on plates near US tests than on other plates, which accounts for almost all of the excess per plate observed around US tests.

### nuc2-title
Judging the remaining ratio against chance

### nuc-null-text
Our test compares observed with expected transients on the plates near nuclear tests, divided by the same ratio on the other plates. The measured ratio is {n:nuc.ratio}, so the expected counts account for almost all of the excess. Fitting the expected counts without the plates near tests, so that those plates cannot shape their own expectation, also gives {n:nuc.held_out}.

For completeness, we still judge the measured ratio against chance. We draw the same number of nights at random, taken from the nights in Palomar’s log. Nights near tests are not a random set, however: the tests came in series, so those nights bunch into a few observing runs, which share batches of emulsion and similar conditions.

As a stricter check that keeps this bunching, we shift the real test calendar by 1 to 5 years and up to two months, leaving out plates taken within two weeks of a real test, and scoring each shifted calendar in the same way. We keep the {n:nuc.calendars} shifted calendars that include about as many plates as the real measurement, to within 30% ({fig:fig.nuclear}).

We also checked that the expected counts would not absorb a genuine excess. We multiplied the number of transients on every plate near a nuclear test by 1.5 and repeated the full measurement, refitting the expected counts with the artificial excess included. The plates near nuclear tests then hold {n:nuc.inj15} times their expected number, and none of the {n:nuc.calendars} shifted calendars gives a ratio as high. An excess of this size, if it were genuine, would be found by our analysis.

The expected counts could also hide a genuine excess if they credited the plates near tests with too many transients. As a check, we judge the excess per plate itself, {n:nuc.raw.ratio}, against the same shifted calendars, with no expected counts at all. The shifted calendars give a median of {n:nuc.raw.cal_median}, and reach {n:nuc.raw.ratio} or more about {n:nuc.raw.p_calendar_t}. They do not keep the season of the real calendar, however: {n:nuc.raw.spring_median} of a typical shifted calendar’s plates were taken between February and June, against {n:nuc.raw.spring_real} of the plates near tests. Across the shifted calendars, the expected counts follow the excess per plate, with a correlation of {n:nuc.raw.corr} between their logarithms.

The plates near tests point at sky where the expected counts are {n:nuc.raw.exp_real} times those of the other plates, which shifted calendars match about {n:nuc.raw.share_exp_t}. The excess per plate therefore follows where the plates near tests pointed, and the expected counts account for almost all of it even when fitted without those plates.

With the ratings of VASCO-ML retrained in the eight ways described above (see Earth’s shadow section), in place of the released ratings, the ratio near nuclear tests ranges from {n:ml.nuc_min} to {n:ml.nuc_max}, which shifted calendars reach or exceed between {n:ml.nuc_p_max} and {n:ml.nuc_p_min}.

### nuclear-caption
Shifted US test calendars give a ratio at least as high as the measured one around {n:nuc.p_calendar_t}. The measured ratio is thus found to be consistent with chance.

### nuclear-verdict
Plates taken near nuclear tests show no statistically significant excess of transients beyond their expected counts. Once each plate’s expected count is taken into account, the ratio of observed to expected transients on these plates, relative to the other plates, is {n:nuc.ratio}. Shifted calendars of US tests give a ratio at least this high about {n:nuc.p_calendar_t}, and random nights about {n:nuc.p_random_t}.

With a window of one day either side of each test in Universal Time, {n:nuc.sym.plates_near} plates fall near tests and the ratio is {n:nuc.sym.ratio}, which shifted calendars match or exceed about {n:nuc.sym.p_calendar_t}. With the list of US tests in VASCO’s code, which leaves out Operation Upshot–Knothole, {n:nuc.vlist.plates_near} plates fall near tests and the ratio is {n:nuc.vlist.ratio}, which shifted calendars match or exceed about {n:nuc.vlist.p_calendar_t}.
