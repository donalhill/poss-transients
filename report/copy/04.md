### cat-h2
Number of transients varies greatly across plates

### cat-intro
The {n:cat.plates} plates in the catalogue show a large degree of variation in how many transients they exhibit, ranging from {n:cat.min} to {n:cat.max} ({fig:fig.sky}).

### sky-caption
Each tile shown represents one plate, coloured on a logarithmic scale according to the number of transients in its central 6 x 6 degree area. Plates along the Milky Way hold fewer transients, as do plates close to a patch near Taurus and Perseus.

### spread-title
More variation than expected by random chance

### spread-text
VASCO’s Earth shadow test (see below) scores its result by treating every transient as an independent event: each has the same small chance of falling inside the shadow, regardless of other transients.

If transients were truly independent of one another and spread uniformly over the sky, plates would differ only by counting noise. An average plate holds {n:cat.avg} transients, and the counting noise on that is around its square root ({n:cat.noise}), so most plates would hold between {n:cat.band_lo} and {n:cat.band_hi} transients.

However, only {n:cat.in_band} of the {n:cat.plates} plates fall within this range ({fig:fig.vasco_spread}). The actual counts spread smoothly, from a handful of transients to several hundred, and on {n:cat.over1000} plates more than 1,000. Some of this variation follows from where each plate points on the sky, as the sky map above shows.

How many transients a plate holds depends on the plate itself, and thus transients cannot be treated as fully independent events. A count drawn from only a few plates, such as the {n:sh.geo.plates} reached by Earth’s shadow, can therefore vary considerably more than counting noise suggests.

### spread-caption
Transients per plate in VASCO’s catalogue. If plates differed only by counting noise, almost all of them would fall in the shaded range about the average; only {n:cat.in_band} of the {n:cat.plates} plates do.

### edge-title
More transients at plate edges

### edge-text
Across the middle of a plate, transients are spread evenly. Towards the edges they grow denser, reaching over two times the central density ({fig:fig.vasco_edge_map}), and {n:cat.edge_share} of the plates are denser near their edges than at their centres. The control positions that the VASCO team spread uniformly over each plate show no such rise.

A plate’s edges are determined by its size and shape, not by anything in the sky. Wherever the telescope points, real sources have no reason to gather towards the edges of a plate. The observed rise may come from the plates themselves, and how the catalogue was built. A detection counts as a transient only if nothing in the Gaia, Pan-STARRS, or WISE catalogues lies within 5 arcseconds of it (Solano et al. 2022). Towards the edges of a plate, star images blur and their measured positions are less accurate, so real stars may miss that match and enter the catalogue as transients. Watters et al. (2026) find the same rise towards plate edges and corners in other lists of transients, and find that transients clump together on most plates.

### edge-caption
The density of transients across the plate (above), and by distance from the centre of the plate (below), relative to the density near the centre, with every plate counted equally. Transients grow denser towards the edges, while VASCO’s control positions, spread uniformly over each plate, stay level. The band shows the 68% range when the plates are resampled.

### crowd-title
Fewer transients near the Milky Way

### crowd-text
A point of light is kept as a transient only if no modern catalogue lists anything within 5 arcseconds. Near the Milky Way, stars are more crowded, causing a greater proportion of candidates to fail the 5 arcsecond threshold.

A typical plate within 5 degrees of the Milky Way holds {n:crowd.inner} transients. Counts rise steadily away from the galactic plane ({fig:fig.vasco_crowd}): beyond 40 degrees, a typical plate holds around {n:crowd.outer} ({n:crowd.ratio} times as many).

### crowd-caption
Transients on each plate against the plate’s distance from the plane of the Milky Way. The line follows the typical plate, and the band holds the middle half of the plates. Plates near the Milky Way hold far fewer transients.

### pointing-title
Where a plate points shapes how many transients it has

### pointing-text
The number of transients retained on a plate depends on where it pointed. Crowding near the Milky Way and dust both remove detections, while plates near the ecliptic, the band of sky that asteroids follow, gain some from asteroids missing from modern lists. These effects come from how the catalogue was built.

Clouds of dust in our own Galaxy dim everything behind them, and the sparse patch near Taurus and Perseus on the sky map ({fig:fig.sky}) lies behind some of the closest dust clouds.

To separate dust from crowding, we compared plates more than 20 degrees from the Milky Way, where stars are not crowded. The {n:dust.n_dusty} plates that look through the most dust hold a median of {n:dust.median_dusty} transients, compared to {n:dust.median_clear} held by the {n:dust.n_clear} plates which look through the least dust. Dust covers only part of the Taurus–Perseus patch, so it cannot explain the whole shortfall there; we have not identified what does.

One further effect is tied to particular plates rather than to the sky. The {n:cat.eq_plates} plates centred on the celestial equator, the southern edge of the catalogue, hold about {n:cat.eq_share} as many transients as other rows of plates once the factors above are accounted for. Where these plates overlap their northern neighbours, the same patch of sky yields only about {n:cat.eq_same_sky} as many transients on the equatorial plates. The cause therefore lies in the plates themselves or how they were processed, not in the sky. The shortfall is already present in the list published by Solano et al. (2022), and is not explained by any of the recorded properties of the plates.

To assign each plate an expected number of transients, we combine these factors in a single fit across all plates, which we call the pointing model: distance from the Milky Way, dust averaged across the plate, distance from the ecliptic, whether the plate lies in the equatorial row, and the share of faint Gaia stars found on the plate’s scan, which measures how deep the plate reaches. Together, these factors account for {n:cat.pointing_share} of the variation in plates’ transient counts, measured on a logarithmic scale ({fig:fig.vasco_pointing}). In the sections on Earth’s shadow and nuclear tests, we use these expected counts to compare plates on equal terms.

### pointing-caption
Transients on each plate against the number the pointing model expects. With the plates grouped by their expected counts, each group’s average count is close to its expected count. Individual plates scatter widely about the model, which accounts for {n:cat.pointing_share} of the variation in plates’ counts on a logarithmic scale.
